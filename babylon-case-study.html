Case Study

Babylon (2022)

How an intentionally lawless environment is actually the most controlled room in the building.

The surface

Bodies, sweat, emotion and performance spilling all over. An elephant, a jazz band and a labyrinth of stimulation. Chaos and unlimited unguilted expression.

To the guests inside, the party looks like it's barely surviving itself. Excess with no ceiling. Noise with no source. Power, flaunted by people who clearly answer to no one.

That's the intended read. It's supposed to look like nothing is holding it together.

It isn't true.

The deconstruction

01

Intent

Every choice starts with one question: how do we immerse a guest as fast as possible? How do we overstimulate and assimilate them into this monster? Every design decision downstream — venue, lighting, sound, scale — exists to answer that question as aggressively as possible.

02

Venue choice / setting the floorplan

No main room. Indoor and outdoor bleed into each other. Sightlines break early and often. Elevation changes keep no single vantage point in control of the whole space. The venue is chosen — or shaped — so that multiple things can happen at once without anyone seeing all of it.

03

Arrival design

The first thing a guest sees is not a greeting — it's motion already in progress. Entry is smooth enough to pull people in, but disorienting enough that they immediately feel behind. The intended read on arrival: it's already happening, and I need to catch up.

04

Scale as intimidation — the elephant

A literal elephant in the room answers one question: how do you establish power instantly? It has to be something obviously impractical, something that proves resources don't matter, something that can't be ignored. A spectacle designed correctly makes the audience see magic while organizers see planted chaos — scale, at this extreme, is a claim of authority made before a single word is spoken.

This is also what retains attention long after the night ends. Guests don't just remember it — they tell people. "I was at this party and they had an elephant." Then, inevitably: "I wish I was at that elephant party." The spectacle doesn't just impress the room it's in. It manufactures demand for the next one.

05

No central focus

There is no main stage. If people look for structure, they don't find it. Multiple performers, overlapping music sources, simultaneous spectacles, no clear hierarchy of importance — the absence of a center is itself the design. People should be constantly missing things.

06

Density and motion — controlled suffocation

Narrow corridors into open air. Furniture and objects used as obstacles, not function. Performers moving through guests, not around them. The questions that shape this: where do people get stuck, when do they spill, where does movement slow. Density isn't an accident of popularity — it's engineered.

07

Lighting — disorientation over visibility

Lighting here is emotional, not functional. Uneven levels, contrast and flickering, shadow pockets that swallow whole areas. The operating principle: I am not helping people see — I am helping them feel lost.

08

Sound design — layers and overlap

Music, crowd noise, ambient sound layered until no single source is identifiable. The goal isn't clarity — it's anticipation. The questions that guide it: what's the dominant sound, when does sound bleed, can guests hear something without ever seeing what made it.

09

Performer vs. guest — the blur

No formal introductions. No applause cues. Performers dressed as guests, guests pulled into moments meant for performers. Nobody can answer "who's working and who's attending" — and that uncertainty is the point. Participation replaces observation.

10

The hidden backbone

Underneath the apparent lawlessness sits absolute control: safety coordination, clear performance cues, reset points, emergency exits, camera choreography. An environment can only feel lawless if that control is invisible, never absent. This layer requires redundant staff, hard stop rules, sober monitors, and clear lines of authority — the parts no guest ever sees, and the parts without which none of the rest survives.

11

Cleanup and reset

The party only works because it can disappear. How fast can it be erased? Who absorbs the damage? What's the exit strategy? The ability to vanish completely isn't just proof of power — it's what creates the future wonder: how did that happen, and will it happen again?

The lesson

Chaos is the aesthetic, not the reality.

Every principle above points to the same two rules. Don't create madness — design systems that appear as madness. Never surrender control, but let others believe you have.

Boldness and recklessness look identical from the outside. The difference is entirely invisible, and entirely the point: prepared people can be bold. Unprepared people are reckless. Babylon's party works because everything that looks unplanned was planned first.

This is the same instinct behind Foundational Considerations and Shaping the environment — just with every dial pushed to its extreme. The backbone is always the part nobody sees. That's the part where I thrive.
